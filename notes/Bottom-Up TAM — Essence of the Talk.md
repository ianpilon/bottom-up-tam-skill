Bottom-Up TAM — Essence of the Talk

  Speaker: Kabir (investment team, This Week in Startups Launch accelerator),
  May 19 2026.

  Core argument

  Top-down TAM ("McKinsey says recruiting is $300B, we'll get 1%") is a black
  box that destroys founder credibility. Bottom-up TAM builds credibility by
  doing the math from your actual pricing and customer count.
  
  The formula

  TAM = Average Contract Value (ACV) × Number of potential customers in your ICP

  Then show the path:
  - Customers needed to hit $10M ARR
  - Customers needed to hit $100M ARR (the venture-scale benchmark — 10x revenue
   for valuation = $1B)

  Why investors care about TAM

  1. Is this venture-scale or a lifestyle business? (Can it reach $100M ARR in
  under 10 years?)
  2. What % of the market do you realistically need to capture? (Needing 50% =
  unrealistic; Tesla is <10% of US cars sold.)
  3. Can it return 50–100x for the fund?

  What NOT to do

  - Wide ranges ("everyone 18–80 in the US")
  - Vague segments ("any B2B SaaS company" — ignores that enterprise ≠ SMB
  sales)
  - Huge round numbers ("$500B TAM") with no math behind them
  - Stacking TAM/SAM/SOM on one slide with no explanation

  Structure of a good TAM pitch

  1. ICP (ideal customer profile) + beachhead market
  2. Go-to-market — specifically how you'll reach them (cold call, channel,
  content, etc.)
  3. The math — ACV × customers = TAM, then customers needed for $10M and $100M
  ARR

  Frame it as a journey, not a destination. Peter Thiel clip: start with a small
   market you can dominate (Amazon → books, PayPal → eBay power sellers, ~20k
  people) and expand from there. "When it's for everyone, it delights no one."
  
  Worked examples in the talk

  - High ACV B2B: $12K ACV × 3M customers = $36B TAM; only 834 customers for
  $10M ARR. Speed-to-scale tilts investors positive.
  - Low ACV B2C: $120/yr × 3.7M = $444M; needs 833K customers — harder, must add
   context (e.g., "22% of beachhead gets us to $100M").
  - Small beachhead: Show geographic expansion (US → UK → Canada) or new
  context (e.g., "22% of beachhead gets us to $100M").
  - Small beachhead: Show geographic expansion (US → UK → Canada) or new products
  to same customers. Justify why you'd expand vs. upsell existing.
  - Multiple ICPs: Justify with conversion data ("31% response rate from
  pediatricians and family practitioners in our cold calls").
  - Multiple price tiers / usage + subscription: Use a weighted average; visually
  downweight the non-venture-scale revenue (e.g., usage-based at <5%) so investors
   focus on the sticky SaaS line.
  - Marketplace: Estimate transactions, not customers. Users × transactions/year ×
   take rate. Introduce both sides (supply + demand) in the story.

  Key Q&A takeaways

  - What if your bottom-up TAM comes out huge ($60B)? Fine, if the assumptions
  (pricing, customer count, transaction frequency) are credible. Credibility, not
  size, is the game.
  - When to introduce market expansion? Late. Startups are resource-constrained.
  Hims & Hers waited ~10 years before international; Duolingo only recently added
  math/chess/music. Don't "boil the ocean." Phrase expansion cautiously ("after we
   dominate vertical X, the natural next vertical is Y because…").
  - Blue-ocean markets (e.g., autonomous-vehicle traffic control): bottom-up is
  harder; you must ground assumptions in concrete narrative about why the market
  will exist.
  - Multiple ICPs vs. one ICP with multiple stakeholders: If two roles must agree
  inside the same buyer org, that's one ICP (the company) with two decision-makers
   — not two ICPs.
  - Cite McKinsey-style market figures = hard no. Black box, kills credibility.

  The one-line takeaway

  Don't tell investors how big the market is. Show them, with your own arithmetic,
   how few customers at your real price get you to $100M ARR — and why those
  specific customers are reachable.
